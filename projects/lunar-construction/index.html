ROBOTIC SPACE CONSTRUCTION

Projecting landmarks for lunar robots.

We project identifiable points onto uneven terrain and use their known 3D positions to help a robot locate itself.

Robotic Localization via Active Projected Fiducials for Space Construction

Mengjun Wang1 · Shuai Li1 · Yunlan Zhang2

Journal of Computing in Civil Engineering · 2026

1 Department of Civil & Coastal Engineering, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL, USA

2 Department of Civil, Architectural and Environmental Engineering, The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX, USA

On the Moon, position estimates can drift.

Without GPS, a rover estimates its motion using onboard sensors, cameras, and maps. Wheel slip can accumulate error, while shadows, dust, and changes to the ground can make natural features difficult to match.

For construction, positioning errors also affect where a robot digs, grades, or places materials.

When natural landmarks are not dependable, a rover needs another reference it can recognize.

Create landmarks where the work happens.

Known reference points can help a rover correct its position. But physical markers and beacons require equipment to be deployed and registered.

We instead project identifiable points directly onto the work surface, without installing a physical marker at every reference location.

The challenge is knowing exactly where each projected point lands.

Offline, calibrated projector rays are intersected with a registered 3D terrain model to determine the coordinates of the projected points.

Online, the rover's camera detects the points, decodes their identities, and matches them to known 3D coordinates to estimate its position and orientation.

How accurately can the rover locate itself?

We evaluated the method in controlled simulations with flat and uneven terrain.

Under nominal conditions, mean translation error ranged from 3.0 to 5.2 mm, with successful localization across all baseline test settings.

The terrain model also matters: on uneven ground, using the actual 3D terrain geometry produced much lower localization error than treating the surface as flat.

When the ground changes, the reference must change.

Excavation and grading change the terrain. Even when the projector remains fixed, the same projected light can land at a different position on the ground.

Future work will focus on updating the terrain model and recalculating landmark positions as construction progresses.

This could eventually help multiple construction robots use a consistent spatial reference while working in the same area.
