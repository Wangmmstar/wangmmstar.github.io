THE CHALLENGE

Construction erases its own landmarks.

Excavation and grading reshape the ground. On the Moon, where GNSS is unavailable, robots need a local reference that remains useful as the worksite changes.

THE IDEA

Instead of finding landmarks,
create them.

A projector places identifiable points directly on the work surface. A robot can observe these temporary landmarks without installing physical markers across the site.

THE INSIGHT

A point of light needs a place in 3D.

A projected pixel does not tell the robot where a point lands on uneven ground. We intersect calibrated projector rays with a registered terrain model to assign each point a 3D worksite coordinate. The robot matches camera observations to these coordinates to recover its pose.

Project light. → Register to terrain. → Recover robot pose.

THE EVIDENCE

Accurate pose recovery in simulation.

3–5 mm

Mean translation error under controlled nominal simulation conditions.

Bench-scale tests also recovered valid poses across 18 viewpoint–lighting acquisitions.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

What would it take to support an entire worksite?

Our current study investigates robot localization using terrain-registered projected landmarks. Extending this approach to active construction raises two challenges.

01 — Update the reference as the ground changes.

Reconstruct altered terrain and recompute the 3D positions of projected landmarks after excavation.

02 — Keep moving robots localized.

Combine observations of projected landmarks with onboard localization to correct accumulated drift.

One worksite.
A common spatial reference.

A shared terrain-based reference could allow rovers, excavators, and other construction robots to operate within the same coordinate frame.
