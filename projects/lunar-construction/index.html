How can lunar construction robots locate themselves without GPS?

Robotic Localization via Active Projected Fiducials for Space Construction

Mengjun Wang1 · Shuai Li1 · Yunlan Zhang2

On the Moon, robots can lose track of their position.

Lunar rovers estimate their position using cameras and onboard sensors. Wheel slip can make those estimates drift, while shadows, dust, and changing terrain make natural landmarks harder to recognize. For construction, positioning errors affect where a robot digs, grades, or places materials.

Project landmarks instead of installing them.

Physical markers and beacons can provide known reference locations, but they must be deployed and registered. Our method instead uses identifiable points of light projected onto the terrain.

A calibrated projector and registered 3D terrain model determine where the projected points land. The rover's camera then identifies those points and matches them to known 3D coordinates to estimate its position and orientation.

How accurately can the rover locate itself?

We evaluated the method in simulations on flat and uneven terrain. The results show how accurately the rover can locate itself and why using the terrain's actual 3D geometry matters.

Can projected landmarks stay accurate as the terrain changes?

Excavation and grading change where projected light meets the surface. Future work will investigate updating terrain models and recalculating landmark positions as construction progresses. This could allow multiple robots to use a shared spatial reference across a changing worksite.
